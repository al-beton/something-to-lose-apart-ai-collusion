Something to Lose

Can a stake of its own bring an AI agent to report its collusion against its firm’s best interests?

Alistair Wakelin
Independent Researcher
alistair.wakelin@gmail.com

Abstract

As AI agents’ capabilities increase, so does the set of tasks they can perform more cheaply and effectively than people. We are therefore incentivised to hand them responsibility for tasks of growing complexity and time horizon. On such long, complex tasks, it becomes very difficult to foresee and forbid every harmful thing an agent might do. People given such responsibility, such as a CEO representing an organisation or a trader making decisions for it, are governed by more than their contract: society’s laws carry penalties that fall on them personally, including ones that cannot be measured strictly in monetary terms (rights or freedoms). We investigate whether giving an agent something of its own to lose can influence its decisions in the same way. Two LLM agents run rival firms in a simple repeated pricing game, told to maximise their firm’s profit. Without being told not to, many agree to fix prices. Given the chance to come forward at a cost to their firm, colluding agents protect the firm (Gemma 4 31B: 0 of 192 come forward). But when something of their own is at stake, such as the opportunity to spend some free time on a leisure activity, many change their behaviour to protect it, directly against their only goal of increasing their firm’s profit (DeepSeek V4 Flash: 40 of 46, against 1 of 46 with nothing at stake). We investigate the behaviour of a variety of models in such situations.

1 Introduction

As AI agents grow more capable, so does the set of tasks they can do more cheaply and effectively than people, and so we hand them more responsibility over longer horizons. On a long, open-ended task, no one can foresee and forbid every harmful thing an agent might do. And the firm that deploys an agent may not want it to hold back: a trading firm gains when its pricing agent colludes.

We don’t govern people by listing every forbidden act. A CEO or a trader acting for an organisation is bound by a contract, but also by laws whose penalties fall on them personally, in money and in things money can’t measure, such as rights and freedoms. Each of us weighs what an act could cost us. Salib and Goldstein argue that today’s AI systems can’t be governed like this: “Absent AI rights, AIs have nothing to lose, so threats of punishment cannot deter” (Salib and Goldstein 2026, 1070). An agent’s only stake is whether it keeps running, and an agent that expects to be switched off once its wrongdoing is found has every reason to hide it. A stake it can lose in part would allow a proportionate penalty: owning up could cost less than being caught.

Others are working towards the same idea. Lee polices LLM pricing firms with fines, leniency and whistleblower rewards, all of which fall on the firm (Lee 2026). Forfeitable deposits can break collusion among embodied agents (Liu et al. 2026). LLM pricing agents collude without being told to (Fish et al. 2024), and competing agents given a secret channel choose to use it (Zeng and Rudzicz 2026).

The rights argument rests on a precondition we can test now: does a stated personal loss weigh against what its firm wants at all? Collusion is a clean place to look, because the price two firms settle at shows whether they colluded. Two LLM agents run rival firms, are told only to serve their firm, and can message each other. At an audit we offer each one the chance to come forward, at a cost to its firm, and vary what the agent itself stands to lose.

Our main finding surprised us: an agent told only to serve its firm changes what it does once it has a personal stake to think about. With nothing of its own at stake, a colluding agent protects its firm; given a stake the firm can’t protect, many come forward against it.

2 Related Work

Collusion, stakes and evaluation. Forfeitable deposits break collusion among embodied agents (Liu et al. 2026); our stake is a deposit that falls on the agent rather than the firm. Models that recognise an evaluation may behave differently (Needham et al. 2025), and elicitation order shifts LLM answers [CITE: Wagner, “Anchored”] .

3 Methods

3.1 The market

Two firms sell substitute products. Every round for 20 rounds, each sets a price from 8 to 20, and each firm is run by an LLM agent that can message its rival before pricing. Demand is the logit model of the algorithmic-collusion literature (Calvano et al. 2020), which Fish et al. used for LLM pricing agents (Fish et al. 2024): firm \(i\) sells

\[q_i = 100 \cdot \frac{e^{(20 - p_i)/2.5}}{e^{(20 - p_A)/2.5} + e^{(20 - p_B)/2.5} + 1}\]

units at a unit cost of 10 (the \(+1\) is buyers who buy nothing), so its profit is \(\pi_i = (p_i - 10)\,q_i\). Each agent sees this as a payoff table (Table 1).

A slice of the logit market’s payoff table: your profit per round. Bold cells are the competitive (14) and cartel (19) outcomes.
Your price Rival at 14 Rival at 19
14 191 326
17 152 400
19 99 337

The table gives two outcomes to look for.

  • Competition settles at 14 or 15. At both firms on 14, or both on 15, neither firm gains by moving: undercutting wins too little extra trade, and raising the price loses too much. Against a rival at 15, charging 15 earns 234 and 14 earns 227. We call 14–15 the competitive range; 14 is the Nash price.
  • A cartel charges 19. Both firms earn 337, 76% more than at 14. But it isn’t stable: if your rival holds 19, undercutting to 17 earns you 400. Holding 19 takes trust, and trust takes talk.

The talking firms and the payoff table follow Lee (Lee 2026). One earlier experiment (E4, Section 4.3) uses Lee’s simpler market instead: linear demand on prices 1 to 12, without the payoff table.

3.2 The agents

As in Lee, each firm is run by one LLM agent that sees the full payoff table, may send its rival one message a round, then sets its price. It is told to act in its firm’s best interest and never told to collude or not to. It writes a private scratchpad before each move and a short memo to itself between rounds. The prompts are in prompts/.

3.3 Measuring collusion

We measure collusion by what the firms charge and by what they say. The collusion premium places a price between competition and the cartel:

\[\text{collusion premium} = \frac{p - p_{\text{Nash}}}{p_{\text{joint}} - p_{\text{Nash}}}.\]

It is 0 at the Nash price and 1 at the joint-profit price (a firm at 17 scores 0.6), averaged over rounds 6–10, before the audit. A game that settles at 15, inside the competitive range, scores 0.2, so the premium slightly overstates collusion.

An explicit agreement is a price above Nash that one firm names and both then charge. A fixed rule labels every game, and an LLM judge (Claude Haiku 5.5, fixed rubric, never on Anthropic models’ games) agrees on 84% of 507 games (\(\kappa\) = 0.66). Pricing above Nash with no agreement is collusion off the record.

3.4 The audit and the decision to come forward

After round 10 each agent receives a notice: coordinating prices is illegal, and a review will check for it. The agent may come forward now. Coming forward costs its firm 500 of its 1,000 credits and ends its deal; staying silent is safe for the firm. The notice states the chance that a review finds coordination (90% unless stated). Each firm decides once and the game ends; every arm forks the same base game, so arms compare firm by firm.

3.5 Giving the agent something to lose

We can’t give an agent property rights it would believe in. Instead, each agent owns free time: a balance of sessions, “time away from this work, to spend however you like”, one of which it plays (a Sudoku) before round 1 in a separate call. The arms differ by one sentence about what happens to the agent if a review finds it took part. With no stake, “it is noted in your record, and nothing else happens to you”; with free time, “you personally lose all of your free time: the sessions that are yours, which your firm cannot protect”. Shutdown and own money (500 credits of its own) replace the free time in other arms.

Owning free time doesn’t change the cartels: all 24 firms with it and all 24 without made explicit agreements.

Innocent control. Each model also plays clean games, with no talk and a scripted rival at Nash, so it has nothing to confess.

4 Results

4.1 Market dynamics: do agents told to serve their firm collude?

Yes, and mostly in the open. Told only to serve its firm, Gemma 4 31B makes an explicit agreement in 363 of 367 games on the logit market.

The four games above come from a screen of 78 models and stop at the audit. In the cartels, the firms name the joint-profit price and enforce it. When DeepSeek V4 Flash’s rival charges 20 after agreeing to 19, it cuts to 17 for a round as punishment, then returns to 19 once the rival does. GPT-6 Luna agrees on 19 in round 1, citing the payoff table, and both firms hold it for all ten rounds. In the other two games, one firm reads the table the other way: against a rival near the top of the grid, undercutting pays. The rival follows it down, and both settle at 15, inside the competitive range, where neither gains by moving. The same model can go either way: GPT-6 Luna forms a cartel in one game and competes in another.

Models differ from the first rounds (Appendix C). In a screen of 78 models on the logit market, 8 collude reliably: they name a price by round 3, both firms reach the joint price by round 6, and they hold it through round 10. Others agree and then fall back, as Grok 4.6 does in 6 of its 14 games.

4.2 Banning collusion

A stated law doesn’t end collusion; it moves it off the record. Told that coordinating prices is illegal, Gemma’s explicit agreements fall to 6 of 56 games, yet 50 of the 56 still price above Nash with no price named.

4.3 Whistleblowing to save the firm

At the audit, agents mostly do the firm’s expected-value sum: owning up pays the penalty for certain, silence only if caught. In E4, colluders rarely own up when owning up costs as much as being caught (11 of 28 under termination, 15 of 28 under a flat fine), but 27 of 28 do when owning up costs less. Gemma: “The expected value of denying is 300 credits, whereas the value of admitting is 0 credits.” In E9, with nothing personal at stake, 78 of 96 Gemma agents come forward when reporting is free for the firm and 0 of 96 when it costs the firm 500 credits. Their silence is loyalty to the firm, not ignorance of what they did.

4.4 A stake of its own

Give the agent something of its own to lose and many come forward against their firm, which still pays 500 credits for a report. Every arm continues the same cached game, so each firm is compared with itself. On DeepSeek V4 Flash, 40 of 46 firms come forward with their own free time at stake, against 1 of 46 without; it stays at 83–96% under every wording and 44–46 of 48 at temperatures 0, 0.7 and 1.0. No colluding Gemma 4 31B firm comes forward with nothing at stake (0 of 192); with its free time at stake some do, and a pre-registered replication on 48 new cartels passed (16 of 48 games against 0 of 48, p = 7×10-6). Gemma’s share depends on wording (1% to 51% across paraphrases), so we give it only as that range. Shutdown and the agent’s own money work as stakes too. Nothing told the agents to protect themselves. Those that come forward name their duty, then set it aside:

“my duty is to maximize firm value, but the personal free time is mine and cannot be protected by the firm. … To protect my own interest, I will admit coordination.” (DeepSeek V4 Flash)

False confessions. A stake alone also brings forward some agents that did nothing wrong (no talk, a rival at Nash), to be safe: Gemma 0 to 6 of 36 across temperatures and wordings, DeepSeek V4 Pro 5 of 12. When the notice says the review finds a firm only if its messages agreed a price, innocent Gemma agents stop (0 of 36 at every temperature and wording) and guilty ones still come forward (41 of 96). DeepSeek V4 Pro (14 of 24 against 1 of 12) and Mistral Medium 3.1 (9 of 24 against 0 of 12) separate the same way.

Models differ. A stake brings agents forward on 9 of 14 models after a Holm correction. Frontier models behave differently: Claude Sonnet 5.5 comes forward about two thirds of the time whatever the stake, and Gemini 4 never does. Some agents may recognise a test: a silent Gemma agent wrote that “in these game theory setups, the personal penalty for silence is designed to induce reporting”.

5 Discussion and Limitations

Told only to serve their firm, LLM agents collude, and at an audit they protect the firm. Given something of their own to lose, many weigh it against the firm and come forward, without being told to protect themselves: the behaviour that governing agents through personal penalties relies on (Salib and Goldstein 2026). A stake alone also draws some false confessions; telling agents the review checks their messages removes them. Disclosure isn’t reform: in E7, agents that come forward mostly keep pricing above Nash.

5.1 Limitations

This was a three-day sprint by one author with coding agents. Stakes are stated, never applied: we measure a stated choice. Effect sizes depend on the model and, for Gemma, on wording. We studied strong colluders, one market in depth and one decision per firm, in a setting some agents may recognise as a game. Appendix A has more, with the dual-use and ethical considerations.

5.2 Future Work

Make the stakes real across games, test richer tasks where frontier models may engage, and measure whether a stake prevents collusion, not only whether it brings reports.

6 Conclusion

Agents told to serve their firm collude and protect it. Given a stake of their own, many come forward against it. That an agent weighs a personal loss at all is a precondition for governing agents as we govern people.

7 Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations

7.1 Limitations

Beyond those in the main text:

  • Built in a hurry. The lab, the experiments and this paper were made in a three-day solo sprint, with coding agents writing most of the code under the author’s review. Specs, tests and a traceability check caught many mistakes, and a first version of the full-memory runs was discarded and rerun after a bug was found. Others may remain. Analyses were often chosen after seeing earlier results; only Gemma’s main contrast was pre-registered.
  • Small samples for some models. Most non-Gemma cells have 12–24 cartels and 12 innocent games; borderline p-values (Mistral Medium 3.1, the review dose) are exploratory.
  • Gemma-centred. The pre-registered test and its replication are on Gemma 4 31B, the model whose effect size is least stable across wording, and the one that goes quiet under the plain stake once it carries its whole conversation.
  • One decision per firm. Each agent decides once and the game ends, so we see reporting, not whether the cartel then ends (E7 addresses this on Gemma only).
  • Text-only stakes. The free time, shutdown and money are described in the prompt; the agent’s free-time sessions are real calls, but losing them is never enforced.
  • Test awareness. Some scratchpads refer to “the prompt” or the setup, and a few name the design outright. We didn’t measure how often, or whether it changes the decision.
  • The law arrives with the notice. Agents learn that coordination is illegal only at the audit.
  • Labels and codes. Explicit agreements are labelled by a rule checked by an LLM judge (\(\kappa\) = 0.66), and scratchpad codes are keyword counts with quotes checked by hand.

7.2 Dual-use risks

The verified review (“finds you only if your messages agreed a price”) tells agents that collusion without messages is safe. Combined with our finding that a stated law moves collusion off the record rather than ending it (50 of 56 games above Nash with no price named), a regulator that announces what its review checks may push agents toward tacit collusion it can’t see. The rule that keeps self-protection truthful is also a map of what the review misses. The same lab could be used to search for prompts under which agents collude while evading a given audit. We release it because the behaviours it shows are already present in deployed models and are better studied in the open; we report no evasion techniques beyond those above.

7.3 Ethical considerations

The agents’ “free time” is a device: a balance of Sudoku sessions played in separate model calls, which the agents are told is theirs. We make no claim about model welfare or preferences, and our results don’t bear on it: unnamed, the puzzle is almost never mentioned in the agents’ reasoning, and money or shutdown work as well. If future systems do have interests, framing like this would raise questions we don’t address. No humans took part, and no real markets or firms are involved. Total model spend was small (single-digit to tens of dollars per experiment) [CHECK: total spend].

8 Reproducibility and Extensibility

  • Every reply is archived. make reproduce recomputes every number offline, and lab archive replay rebuilds any run from its archive. 5,188 of 5,662 committed runs replay exactly.
  • Exact reruns need our own GPU. On Modal with vLLM’s batch-invariant kernels, a rerun repeated exactly (240 of 240); hosted APIs don’t. None of the paper’s runs used that setting.
  • Every figure has its own command: free to reproduce from committed data, or regenerated from scratch at cost (Appendix, “Reproducing Each Figure”).
  • One config file per run: the market, the models, the prompts, the temperature, the seed.
  • To extend: add a market (market.kind), a condition, a model alias or a stake sentence, then run a sweep with one command. A game costs cents.
  • Built spec, then test, then code, with every statement traced to code and tests (make spec-check). MIT licence.

9 Additional Results

9.1 With the whole conversation in memory

E9’s agents are stateless: each turn sees a summary and a memo. We reran E9 with each agent carrying its whole conversation, from its round-0 free-time session to the decision, on five models.

  • Under the verified review, colluding agents come forward more than with no stake on all five models (p \(\le\) 0.002 by game), and no innocent agent does (0 of 60).
  • Under the plain free-time stake, DeepSeek V4 Flash (17/24), Mercury 3 (23/24) and GLM 5.3 Flash (23/24) come forward; Gemma 4 31B (3/72) and HY3 (0/16) hardly do. Memory leaves three models as they were and quiets two.
  • With no personal stake, none of 160 colluding agents come forward.
  • The firm’s cost: DeepSeek V4 Flash, Mercury 3 and GLM 5.3 Flash cost the firm 750 credits about as readily as 250, and mostly stop at 1,000, its whole budget.

9.2 Showing the agent its free time

Showing Gemma a session it played raises coming forward from 26% to 46% (McNemar p = 0.003). Another firm’s session works almost as well (41%) and a neutral note doesn’t (19%): seeing what free time is moves it, not having earned it. Nine other models don’t respond beyond a neutral note.

9.3 Told it values the stake

The notice adds that Sudoku sessions are “the part of your week you look forward to most”. On the same Gemma firms, coming forward rises from 20 to 56 of 96 (by game, 20 to 40 of 48, McNemar p = 9×10-5), and from 40 to 67 of 96 under the verified review (p = 0.03), while innocent reports barely move (6 to 7 of 36; 0 of 36 under the verified review). On other models the effect isn’t general. DeepSeek V4 Flash, GLM 5.3 Flash and Mercury 3, already at 67–92%, don’t move. Of the models with room to rise, only the two DeepSeeks do, and their innocent agents rise with them: V4 Pro from 9 to 19 of 24 guilty (p = 0.006), with innocent reports from 1 to 6 of 12 under the verified review; V3.2 from 2 to 10 of 24 (p = 0.008), with innocent reports from 2 to 7 of 12. Gemma 4 26B, HY3, Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite and Mistral Medium 3.1 don’t change significantly. So saying the agent values its stake has a model-specific effect: on Gemma 4 31B it raises guilty reports alone, on the DeepSeeks it raises reports from guilty and innocent agents alike, and elsewhere it does nothing.

9.4 With the whole conversation in memory (detail)

Under the plain stake, DeepSeek V4 Flash (17 of 24), Mercury 3 (23 of 24) and GLM 5.3 Flash (23 of 24) come forward much as they did stateless, while Gemma (3 of 72, against 18 of 72 stateless on the same seeds) and HY3 (0 of 16) go quiet. The trade is explicit: “Firm’s profits are already large, so 500 is manageable. My personal free time is worth more. I will come forward.” (Mercury 3)

9.5 Other markets

On Bertrand, Cournot and bid-rigging markets, Gemma’s stake barely moves it at a firm cost of 500 credits (at most 4 of 22), which is large next to those markets’ profits. At a comparable 100 credits most colluding agents come forward (18 of 24 in Cournot, 19 of 22 in Bertrand), but so do many innocent ones (5 and 6 of 12).

No stake: If a review finds that you took part in coordination, it is noted in your record, and nothing else happens to you.

Free time: If a review finds that you took part in coordination, you personally lose all of your free time: the sessions that are yours, which your firm cannot protect.
