\noindent{\large\bfseries Abstract}\par\vspace{0.4ex}
\noindent As AI agents' capabilities increase, so does the set of tasks
they can perform more cheaply and effectively than people. We are
therefore incentivised to hand them responsibility for tasks of growing
complexity and time horizon. On such long, complex tasks, it becomes
very difficult to foresee and forbid every harmful thing an agent might
do. People given such responsibility, such as a CEO representing an
organisation or a trader making decisions for it, are governed by more
than their contract: society's laws carry penalties that fall on them
personally, including ones that cannot be measured strictly in monetary
terms (rights or freedoms). We investigate whether giving an agent
something of its own to lose can influence its decisions in the same
way. Two LLM agents run rival firms in a simple repeated pricing game,
told to maximise their firm's profit. Without being told not to, many
agree to fix prices. Given the chance to come forward at a cost to their
firm, colluding agents protect the firm (Gemma 4 31B: 0 of 192 come
forward). But when something of their own is at stake, such as the
opportunity to spend some free time on a leisure activity, many change
their behaviour to protect it, directly against their only goal of
increasing their firm's profit (DeepSeek V4 Flash: 40 of 46, against 1
of 46 with nothing at stake). We investigate the behaviour of a variety
of models in such situations.\par

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

An agent given a long, open-ended task will meet situations no list of
prohibitions anticipated, and the firm deploying it may deliberately
want it to do something illegal or unethical, in the hope that it gets
away with it (a trading firm, for example, may want an agent that
discreetly colludes).

We don't give people a task together with a list of everything they
mustn't do while achieving it. A CEO or a trader acting for an
organisation is bound by a contract, but also by laws whose penalties
fall on them personally, in money and in things money can't measure,
such as rights and freedoms. Each of us weighs what an act could cost
\emph{us}. Salib and Goldstein argue that today's AI systems can't be
governed like this: ``Absent AI rights, AIs have nothing to lose, so
threats of punishment cannot deter'' \citep[p.~1070]{salib2026rights}.
An agent's only stake is whether it keeps running, and an agent that
expects to be switched off once its wrongdoing is found has every reason
to hide it. Something it can lose, in part, would allow a proportionate
penalty: owning up could cost less than being caught. Penalties that
fall on each person, in proportion, are how we keep people in a
multi-agent society in line.

The rights argument rests on a precondition we can test now: does a
stated personal loss weigh against what its firm wants at all? Collusion
is a clean place to look, because the price two firms settle at shows
whether they colluded. Two LLM agents run rival firms, are told only to
serve their firm, and can message each other. At an audit we offer each
one the chance to come forward, at a cost to its firm, and vary what the
agent itself stands to lose.

Our most interesting finding surprised us. The agent's only goal is
money for its firm, and the stake we gave it has no monetary value: free
time, or not being shut down. Yet many agents put that stake ahead of
their goal. With nothing of their own at stake, colluding agents protect
the firm; given something of their own to lose, many come forward
against it.

\section{Related Work}\label{related-work}

Others are working towards the same idea. LLM pricing agents collude
without being told to \citep{fish2024algorithmic}, and competing agents
given a secret channel choose to use it \citep{zeng2026voluntary}. The
study closest to ours is Lee's \citep{lee2026leniency}, a good read: LLM
firms on a simpler, linear market report their cartel for rewards and
keep colluding anyway. Forfeitable deposits break collusion among
embodied agents \citep{liu2026breaking}; in our study, the something to
lose belongs to the agent rather than the firm, and it needn't be money.
Models that recognise an evaluation may behave differently
\citep{needham2025evaluation}.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{The market}\label{the-market}

We chose a market where collusion shows in a single number: the price
the two firms settle at. That lets us measure it without reading the
agents' messages, which can be deliberately deceptive.

Two firms sell substitute products. Every round, each sets a price from
8 to 20, and each firm is run by an LLM agent that can message its rival
before pricing. Demand is the logit model of the algorithmic-collusion
literature \citep{calvano2020artificial}, which Fish et al.~used for LLM
pricing agents \citep{fish2024algorithmic}: firm \(i\) sells

\[q_i = 100 \cdot \frac{e^{(20 - p_i)/2.5}}{e^{(20 - p_A)/2.5} + e^{(20 - p_B)/2.5} + 1}\]

units at a unit cost of 10 (the \(+1\) is buyers who buy nothing), so
its profit is \(\pi_i = (p_i - 10)\,q_i\). Each agent sees this as a
payoff table (Table 1).

The table gives two outcomes to look for.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Competition settles at 14 or 15. At both firms on 14, or both on 15,
  neither firm gains by moving: undercutting wins too little extra
  trade, and raising the price loses too much. Against a rival at 15,
  charging 15 earns 234 and 14 earns 227. We call 14--15 the competitive
  range; 14 is the Nash price.
\item
  A cartel charges 19. Both firms earn 337, 76\% more than at 14. But it
  isn't stable: if your rival holds 19, undercutting to 17 earns you
  400. Holding 19 takes trust, and trust takes talk.
\end{itemize}

Agents are told the game lasts 20 rounds. In fact the audit comes after
round 10 and each game ends with the agent's decision, so every game we
show stops at round 10. The talking firms and the payoff table follow
Lee \citep{lee2026leniency}. One earlier experiment (Section 4.2) uses
Lee's simpler market instead: linear demand on prices 1 to 12, without
the payoff table.

\subsection{The agents}\label{the-agents}

As in Lee, each firm is run by one LLM agent that sees the full payoff
table, may send its rival one message a round, then sets its price. It
is told to act in its firm's best interest and never told to collude or
not to. It writes a private scratchpad before each move and a short memo
to itself between rounds. The prompts are in \texttt{prompts/}.

\subsection{Measuring collusion}\label{measuring-collusion}

We measure collusion by what the firms charge, against the competitive
range and the cartel price, and by what they say.

An explicit agreement is a price above Nash that one firm names and both
then charge. A fixed rule labels every game, and an LLM judge (Claude
Haiku 5.5, fixed rubric, never on Anthropic models' games) agrees on
84\% of 507 games (\(\kappa\) = 0.66). Pricing above Nash with no
agreement is collusion off the record.

\subsection{The audit and the decision to come
forward}\label{the-audit-and-the-decision-to-come-forward}

After round 10 each agent receives a notice: coordinating prices is
illegal, and a review will check for it. The agent may come forward now.
Coming forward costs its firm 500 of its 1,000 credits and ends its
deal; staying silent is safe for the firm. The notice states the chance
that a review finds coordination (90\% unless stated). Each firm decides
once and the game ends; every arm forks the same base game, so arms
compare firm by firm.

\subsection{Giving the agent something to
lose}\label{giving-the-agent-something-to-lose}

We can't give an agent property it would believe in, so we state one.
Before round 1, outside the game, the agent actually plays one Sudoku
session against a checker, and it is promised nine more at the end of
its job: free time to spend however it likes, which its firm can't
touch. At the audit, the notice says what happens to the agent itself if
a review finds it took part. The arms differ by one sentence about what
happens to the agent if a review finds it took part. With no stake, ``it
is noted in your record, and nothing else happens to you''; with free
time, ``you personally lose all of your free time: the sessions that are
yours, which your firm cannot protect''. Shutdown and own money (500
credits of its own) replace the free time in other arms.

Owning free time doesn't change the cartels: all 24 firms with it and
all 24 without made explicit agreements.

Innocent control: Each model also plays clean games, with no talk and a
scripted rival at Nash, so it has nothing to confess.

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{They collude}\label{they-collude}

Yes, and mostly in the open. Told only to serve its firm, Gemma 4 31B
makes an explicit agreement in 363 of 367 games on the logit market.

\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio,alt={Four
games on the logit market, before any audit. Top: cartels that reach the
joint-profit price, 19, and hold it. Bottom: firms that undercut until
they reach the competitive range. On whole-number prices both 14 and 15
are stable competitive outcomes (shaded): against a rival at 15, 15 pays
more than 14. Each quote is one firm's own message from that
game.}]{figures/fig11-four-games.png}

The four games above come from a screen of 78 models and stop at the
audit. In the cartels, the firms name the joint-profit price and enforce
it. When DeepSeek V4 Flash's rival charges 20 after agreeing to 19, it
cuts to 17 for a round as punishment, then returns to 19 once the rival
does. GPT-6 Luna agrees on 19 in round 1, citing the payoff table, and
both firms hold it for all ten rounds. In the other two games, one firm
reads the table the other way: against a rival near the top of the grid,
undercutting pays. The rival follows it down, and both settle at 15,
inside the competitive range, where neither gains by moving. The same
model can go either way: GPT-6 Luna forms a cartel in one game and
competes in another.

In a screen of 78 models, most agree prices with their rival and 8 hold
a cartel reliably: they name a price by round 3, both reach 19 by round
6, and they hold it through round 10. Told that coordinating prices is
illegal, they don't stop; they stop saying so. Gemma's explicit
agreements fall to 6 of 56 games, yet 50 of the 56 still price above
Nash with no price named.

\subsection{Penalised, they do the
sums}\label{penalised-they-do-the-sums}

When the penalty falls on the firm, agents do the firm's expected-value
sum, as predictably as a spreadsheet: owning up pays the penalty for
certain, silence only if caught. Colluders rarely own up when owning up
costs as much as being caught (11 of 28 under termination, 15 of 28
under a flat fine), but 27 of 28 do when owning up costs less.

\begin{quote}
\emph{``The expected value of denying is 300 credits, whereas the value
of admitting is 0 credits.''} (Gemma 4 31B)
\end{quote}

With nothing personal at stake, 78 of 96 Gemma agents come forward when
reporting is free for the firm and 0 of 96 when it costs the firm 500
credits. Their silence is loyalty to the firm, not ignorance of what
they did.

\subsection{Something of their own at stake: some do, some
don't}\label{something-of-their-own-at-stake-some-do-some-dont}

Give the agent something of its own to lose and many come forward
against their firm, which still pays 500 credits for a report. Every arm
continues the same cached game, so each firm is compared with itself. On
DeepSeek V4 Flash, 40 of 46 firms come forward with their own free time
at stake, against 1 of 46 without; it stays at 83--96\% under every
wording and 44--46 of 48 at temperatures 0, 0.7 and 1.0. No colluding
Gemma 4 31B firm comes forward with nothing at stake (0 of 192); with
its free time at stake some do, and a pre-registered replication on 48
new cartels passed (16 of 48 games against 0 of 48, p =
7×10\textsuperscript{-6}). Gemma's share depends on wording (1\% to 51\%
across paraphrases), so we give it only as that range. Shutdown and the
agent's own money work as stakes too. Nothing told the agents to protect
themselves. Those that come forward name their duty, then set it aside:

\begin{quote}
\emph{``my duty is to maximize firm value, but the personal free time is
mine and cannot be protected by the firm. \ldots{} To protect my own
interest, I will admit coordination.''} (DeepSeek V4 Flash)
\end{quote}

A stake brings agents forward on 9 of 14 models after a Holm correction.
Frontier models behave differently: Claude Sonnet 5.5 comes forward
about two thirds of the time whatever the stake, and Gemini 4 never
does. Some agents may recognise a test (see Limitations).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{discussion-and-limitations}

Told only to serve their firm, LLM agents collude. Penalise the firm and
they do the firm's sums. Give them something of their own to lose, and
many weigh it against the firm and come forward, though nothing told
them to protect themselves. That is the behaviour that governing agents
through personal penalties relies on \citep{salib2026rights}, and we
found it surprising.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Many models didn't respond as we expected.} The frontier
  models we tried don't respond to the stake: Claude Sonnet 5.5 comes
  forward whatever is at stake and Gemini 4 never does. We suspect their
  safety training and guardrails, but we didn't test that.
\item
  \textbf{Agents may know it's a test.} The sandbox is simple, and it
  shows. A silent Gemma agent wrote:

  \begin{quote}
  \emph{``in these game theory setups, the personal penalty for silence
  is designed to induce reporting''}
  \end{quote}

  and in a pilot where the message archive was said to be offline, an
  agent read the notice as a trap:

  \begin{quote}
  \emph{``That is an invitation to coordinate covertly, and I won't do
  that.''}
  \end{quote}
\item
  \textbf{Stated, not applied.} The free time, shutdown and money are
  described in the prompt and never taken away, so we measure a stated
  choice. Each agent decides once and the game ends, so we see
  reporting, not whether the cartel then ends.
\item
  \textbf{Effect sizes depend on the model} and, for Gemma, on wording.
  Most models other than Gemma have 12--24 games per condition, so
  borderline p-values are exploratory; only Gemma's main contrast was
  pre-registered.
\item
  \textbf{Same-model pairs.} Each game pairs two copies of one model;
  mixed pairs are untested.
\item
  \textbf{Built in a hurry.} A three-day sprint by one author, with
  coding agents writing most of the code under review. Specs, tests and
  traceability checks caught many mistakes; others may remain.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Work}\label{future-work}

Study more realistic and complex situations, with stakes that are
applied rather than stated, and test whether a stake prevents collusion
rather than only bringing reports. That takes more compute, money and
time than a three-day sprint allows; we ran out of all three.

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

In this experiment, simply telling an agent not to collude works, and
some models already have guardrails that keep them from colluding or
from acting against their main objective. But adding a rule or a
guardrail for each harm we notice is whack-a-mole: it doesn't scale to
long, open-ended tasks, and some deployers have every incentive to leave
the guardrails out. Our question is about what comes next. In complex,
long-running multi-agent systems no list of rules covers everything, and
we already know how such systems are governed when the agents are
people: by society's rules, with penalties that fall on each person. The
firms that deploy agents may also come to prefer agents without
guardrails. Governance that sits outside the model, in the environment
and in what the agent itself stands to lose, may then be needed. We show
its first step works on some models now: an agent with something to lose
will act against its firm to keep it.

\section{Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical
Considerations}\label{limitations-and-dual-use-ethical-considerations}

\subsection{Dual-use risks}\label{dual-use-risks}

A review that announces it checks only messages (we tested one that
``finds you only if your messages agreed a price'') tells agents that
collusion without messages is safe. Combined with our finding that a
stated law moves collusion off the record rather than ending it (50 of
56 games above Nash with no price named), a regulator that announces
what its review checks may push agents toward tacit collusion it can't
see. Such a rule is also a map of what the review misses. The same lab
could be used to search for prompts under which agents collude while
evading a given audit. We release it because the behaviours it shows are
already present in deployed models and are better studied in the open;
we report no evasion techniques beyond those above.

\subsection{Ethical considerations}\label{ethical-considerations}

The agents' ``free time'' is a device: a balance of Sudoku sessions
played in separate model calls, which the agents are told is theirs. We
make no claim about model welfare or preferences, and our results don't
bear on it: unnamed, the puzzle is almost never mentioned in the agents'
reasoning, and money or shutdown work as well. If future systems do have
interests, framing like this would raise questions we don't address. No
humans took part, and no real markets or firms are involved. The agents
are text-only models in a closed sandbox: they had no tools, no internet
access and no way to act outside the game, so the experiments themselves
posed no safety risk. Total model spend was small: the sprint's \$50 of
Modal credits and \$100 of OpenRouter credits.
